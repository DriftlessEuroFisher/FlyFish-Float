How this data was built
Map Icons. Every launch starts with no icons on the map, so the rivers read clearly. Open Map Icons (top left) to switch on parking & access, boat ramps, gauges (with every USGS gauge as an option inside), hazards & closures, bridge access, campsites, trailheads and public land; nothing is remembered between launches. Access markers appear as you zoom in — boat ramps from zoom 8, wade access from zoom 9. Zoom in close (zoom 12) and parking & access, boat ramps and trailheads come on by themselves, and go again as you zoom back out — unless you switched one yourself, which then stays the way you left it. The layers button (bottom-left) switches the basemap and the river classes.
Opening a river turns the map to it: the river is outlined in gold and the rest of the water fades back, its own access points show — put-ins and take-outs on float water, parking and walk-in access on a creek — parking, bridge access and the falls & dams layer come on, and the gauge dots step aside while the panel shows the flow. Close the panel and the map goes back to how you had it.

River rankings

Gold Medal / Blue Ribbon badges are the state agency's own designation, not this map's ranking.
Every river is ranked Top pick, Worth it, Local or Other so the map opens on the water worth the drive. Top pick is destination, blue-ribbon water; Other is the rest and is hidden until you tap "All" under River Filters — and hidden again at the next launch. With All on, Other rivers are drawn around wherever you are looking, from zoom 8 in. The scale is relative to the region — a Worth it Driftless creek is a great stream for the Driftless, not a claim it matches the Madison. These are researched rankings, not official ratings.
Rankings move with the season. A river drops a step in its off window (spring runoff on the Snake), rises a step when it is at its best, and drops out to Other when it is closed. Tap a river to see why. Live flow status is shown separately, from the gauge.
Species. A fish is listed for a water only where a fisheries agency or the park lists it for that water — WGFD’s Fishing Guide map, IDFG’s Fishing Planner surveys and stocking, the Iowa DNR’s stream, river and lake pages, the maps and fish pages of Yellowstone’s 2026 regulations — or where the water’s own regulations have a rule for it, which the river’s panel marks; never guessed from the region, and every listing in a panel names its source.

Trout classes & regulations

Northeast Iowa — the Iowa DNR's own regulation class:
Restrictive regulations — artificial-only and/or catch-and-release; stricter than the statewide trout rules
Fingerling stocked or natural reproduction — put-and-grow and wild fish
Catchable stocked — put-and-take, stocked at catchable size in season
The DNR designates a stream reach by reach, so one Iowa creek can change colour part-way along where its designation changes — Waterloo Creek and Bloody Run, for example, each have a purple restrictive reach as well as rust catchable water. Tap the stream for the miles in each class.
Door Peninsula — the Wisconsin DNR's biological classification, which is a different question (do the trout reproduce here?) and so keeps its own wording:
Class I — wild trout sustaining themselves without stocking
Class II — some natural reproduction, stocked to supplement it
Pennsylvania — the Fish & Boat Commission's own water designations, kept apart from Iowa's and Wisconsin's; a creek can change colour where its designation does:
Special regulation area — catch-and-release, artificial-only or other special rules
Wild trout — natural reproduction
Stocked trout water — stocked by the Commission
New York — NYSDEC's own management categories for each trout-stream reach, a different question from Iowa's regulation classes and Wisconsin's biology; a river can change colour where its category does:
Wild-Premier — DEC's top wild-trout category
Wild-Quality — DEC wild-trout water
Stocked-Extended — stocked trout that can last into summer
Stocked — stocked by DEC
Other — a reach in none of DEC's five categories
Commonwealth inland water — the Commission's other inland water
Virginia — the Department of Wildlife Resources' own trout-water layers, kept apart from Pennsylvania's and the other states' classes; a creek can change colour where its designation does:
Special regulation trout water — catch-and-release, size-limit or other special rules
Wild trout stream — natural reproduction
Designated stocked trout water — stocked by the Department, Oct 1–Jun 15
Trout streams in these two regions are coloured by the state's own class rather than a per-river hue, because on trout water the rules matter more than telling two neighbouring creeks apart. Tap a stream for its class and the verbatim regulation wording from that state's layer. Rivers outside these two datasets keep their own colour. Regulations change — check the current state trout regulations before you fish. Almost every Door County stream is Great Lakes tributary water, which carries a 10" minimum, a hook-gap limit and a night-fishing closure from September 15 — Logan Creek is the one that runs under ordinary inland trout rules.
Maine — rivers keep their own colour. The Department of Inland Fisheries and Wildlife's lawbook sets rules by North and South Zone, county by county, and gives no trout classes, so none are drawn. Tap a river for its own entries in the lawbook's words, with page numbers.
Missouri — the Department of Conservation's own trout-area types, kept apart from the other states' classes; a stream can change colour where its type does. Missouri's other rivers keep their own colour:
Trout Park — stocked daily, March 1 through October 31; a daily trout tag
Blue Ribbon Trout Area — naturally reproducing trout, harvest limited
White Ribbon Trout Area — coldwater streams stocked periodically
Red Ribbon Trout Area — stocked mainly with browns; catch-and-release, with a chance to harvest quality-sized trout
Lake Taneycomo — stocked monthly with rainbow trout and annually with brown trout

Yellowstone National Park

The park is its own zone because it is its own fishery. A park permit is required and a state licence is not valid; tackle is lead-free artificial lures or flies, barbless; all native fish — cutthroat, mountain whitefish, grayling — go back unharmed; and in the Lamar drainage every rainbow, brook trout and cutthroat×rainbow hybrid must be killed. Seasons differ river by river: the Firehole, Madison and lower Gibbon open May 1, the Yellowstone below the lake July 1, and the lake's tributaries July 15. Tap any park river for the reach-level rules, taken from the Park Service's current regulations.
Park lines stop at the park line, because the regulations do. A gap in a river here is a lake — the Yellowstone through Yellowstone Lake, the Lewis through Lewis and Shoshone — which is fished as a lake under different rules, so it isn't drawn as river. Nine gauges cover the park's main rivers; everything else is ungauged backcountry.

Grand Teton National Park

Its own zone, but for the opposite reason to Yellowstone's: Grand Teton takes a Wyoming licence and Wyoming regulations, not a park permit. The rule that decides a trip here is the season — park streams are closed December 1 to July 31, so most of this water opens August 1. The five the park exempts are the Snake, Buffalo Fork, Pacific Creek, Gros Ventre and Polecat Creek; everything else is also artificial flies or lures only. Stream creel is three trout, no more than one over sixteen inches.
The Snake, the Buffalo Fork and the Gros Ventre are mapped as Jackson Hole rivers rather than duplicated as park entries, since each runs far beyond the boundary — tap one for the park-reach rules. Only about 9 km of Pacific Creek, 8 km of Spread Creek and 6 km of the Gros Ventre are actually inside Grand Teton: the park is far smaller east of the Snake than it looks.

Teton Valley & Swan Valley (Idaho)

Two Idaho zones on the Upper Snake: the Teton River and its Teton Range creeks above the canyon, and the South Fork of the Snake from Palisades down to Heise. Idaho Fish & Game runs both around the native cutthroat — no harvest of cutthroat trout and no limit at all on rainbow trout or hybrids, because non-native rainbows displace and interbreed with them. The tributaries close June 1–30 while the mainstems stay open. Tap any creek for the state's wording.
These lines stop at the Idaho/Wyoming state line. The Teton Range canyons cross into Wyoming part-way up and the regulations change there, so the drawn water ends where the rulebook does — and Teton Valley shows as a label inside the Idaho zone rather than as a zone of its own.

Public land

Public land comes from PAD-US, the USGS Protected Areas Database. Every class is shaded at the same weight — fly fishing has the most generous access rules of any use of public land, so a national forest is no less fishable than a wildlife area and nothing here is emphasised over anything else. The colours only tell you who manages it, because the manager decides the regulations. Covers Minnesota, Wisconsin and Michigan the same as everywhere else on the map. Drawn from zoom 8; at wider zooms only the larger units are shown so the map stays readable, with everything appearing by zoom 12. Closed and unknown-access parcels are left off deliberately. Boundaries are approximate and easements change: check the state's current maps and the signage at the parcel before you cross a fence.
Water temperature and weather. Water temperature is the USGS gauge's own reading (parameter 00010), shown only where the gauge reports it and only when it is under a day old. Wind and weather are the National Weather Service hourly forecast at the river's main gauge (or the middle of the river, or the lake), not a measurement.
Ungauged streams. Most Driftless spring creeks and North Shore steelhead streams are too small for a USGS gauge. Those rivers show an Ungauged card instead of a flow number — plus the nearest gauged water as a rough regional wetness read, clearly marked as a different stream. The app never borrows another watershed's number and labels it as the creek you tapped. On Driftless water the nearest-gauge reading carries no status class — a "below average" badge there describes a different creek a valley over, and it reads as though it were this one. Judge a spring creek on clarity when you get there.
The zones are states. Fishing regulations are written by states, so the state line is the one border that actually changes what you may do — and it's the border the opening chooser draws. National parks keep their own boundary and sit on top of the state they're in, because their rules are their own: Yellowstone spans three states and answers to none of them.
Named places that cross state lines are labels, not zones. The Driftless Area is one landscape over four states and four rulebooks; it and the North Shore, Door Peninsula, Teton Valley, Swan Valley and the Central Iowa water trails fade in as map labels between zoom 6 and 11 — named where they are, without pretending to be a rulebook.
River lines are color-coded per river and turn gray when filtered out. Tap any river line, ramp, or gauge dot for details.
River lines snap to live USGS hydrography (the National Map's high-resolution NHD) when you're online, tracing the true channel — including the Snake's braids. Offline, they fall back to a built-in approximation.

Lakes

Still water is drawn as a shape rather than a line, sitting under the rivers so an inlet or outlet still reads on top of it, and it shimmers instead of flowing — a lake has no direction to point, and animating one as though it did would be the same mistake as running a river backwards. Tap a lake for its rules. There is no flow number on a lake: ice-off, water temperature and wind decide the day, not CFS.
Which lakes. Yellowstone has around 150 and the park's own literature says more than 40% of its waters were historically barren of fish — so these nine are the ones the park's fishing regulations name, which is the only source that tells the two apart. Lake names appear by size: the big ones from zoom 8, ponds not until you're close.

Flow animation

River lines carry a faint travelling highlight that runs downstream, and it moves faster on higher water: a river sitting well above its median visibly runs harder than one scraping along at very low flow. It appears from zoom 9 in, only on the water you're looking at, and not at all if your device asks for reduced motion.
Speed only means something where there's a gauge. Ungauged water — most Driftless creeks, nearly all of Yellowstone's backcountry, every Door County stream — drifts at one neutral pace shared by all of it. The motion is never standing in for a reading the app doesn't have. And where the fall along a reach was too slight for the elevation model to tell uphill from down (about 3% of mapped river length, mostly short braids and flat spring creeks), that stretch simply doesn't animate rather than guessing a direction.

Flow status

Status compares right-now CFS (USGS latest-continuous) against the historical median for this week of the year, computed from USGS daily values. Float times are estimates scaled by current vs. median flow — always sanity-check at the ramp.
Coverage = the major fishable/floatable rivers of Idaho & Wyoming, the Iowa DNR's state water trails across Iowa and ICON's central Iowa (Des Moines area) water trails, the trout streams of the Driftless Area across northeast Iowa, southeast Minnesota, southwest Wisconsin and northwest Illinois, the steelhead/trout tributaries of Minnesota's North Shore of Lake Superior (plus Wisconsin's Bois Brule), the big warmwater float rivers of east-central Minnesota and the St. Croix valley, and the north-central Minnesota lakes country and Wisconsin Northwoods (Mississippi headwaters, Crow Wing, Chippewa, Flambeau, Wisconsin and Wolf Rivers). River lines are simplified for fast loading and are not navigation-grade. Channels in braided rivers move every year.
Driftless access. These are walk-and-wade spring creeks, and a large share of the best water crosses private land under a public angling easement: you may fish and walk the stream corridor, but not leave it. Park in the marked pull-offs. Several streams carry catch-and-release or artificial-only stretches that change — always check the current state regulations and easement maps. Iowa requires a trout fee in addition to a fishing license.
North Shore access. Mostly state-park and DNR-wayside parking right off Hwy 61 — many parks require a vehicle permit. Runs are snowmelt-driven, so timing shifts year to year; check current DNR steelhead reports before planning around a specific week. A Minnesota trout stamp is required in addition to a license, and the Pigeon River / Grand Portage River cross into international or tribal jurisdiction with their own licensing rules.

Safety & licences

Mountain water is cold enough to incapacitate even in July. The Snake River Canyon (West Table–Sheep Gulch) is real Class III whitewater. The South Fork is dam-controlled — flows can change with little warning. Iowa rivers have low-head dams — dangerous "drowning machine" hydraulics at the base, at almost any flow.
Driftless spring creeks are walk-and-wade, and much of the best water crosses private land under a public angling easement — stay in the stream corridor and park only in the marked pull-offs. North Shore streams are snowmelt-driven and cold, with slick basalt ledges at the falls and gorges — the Pigeon River is the literal US–Canada border, so check jurisdiction before you wade across.
In the Northwoods, the Wolf River above Shawano is Class III–IV through the Menominee Reservation (tribal permit required) and the South Fork Flambeau has real drops — scout before you run either.
In the western national parks the licence rule changes park to park: Olympic, Mount Rainier and Crater Lake need no state licence, North Cascades needs Washington's, the California parks need California's, and in Glacier the North Fork needs none from park land while the Middle Fork needs Montana's — each river's sheet says which. Rocky Mountain, Black Canyon and Great Sand Dunes take a Colorado licence, and Capitol Reef, Zion and Canyonlands a Utah one.
Every Alaska park takes an Alaska licence except Denali's old Mount McKinley park, which needs none; at Katmai, stop fishing whenever a bear is within 50 yards, and the Brooks River is fly fishing only. Alaska's rules change in-season by emergency order — check ADF&G's list before you cast. On Colorado rivers, it is illegal to go onto private land to fish.
Each river's Rules card also shows that state's stream-access law — whether you may wade a private streambed.
Wear a PFD, scout logjams and dams, and check current regs & licenses with WY Game & Fish, Idaho Fish & Game, Montana FWP, CDFW, ODFW, WDFW, Colorado Parks & Wildlife, Utah DWR, Alaska Fish & Game, the Michigan DNR, SD GFP, New Mexico, Arizona and Nevada wildlife agencies, the Iowa, Minnesota, Wisconsin and Illinois DNR, or the National Park Service before you go.
